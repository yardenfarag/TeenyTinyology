# Episode 002 — Roly & Mango (the tongue-eating louse)

*Cymothoa exigua*, a cymothoid isopod, and the spotted rose snapper *Lutjanus guttatus*.

## The idea in five beats
1. **Hook.** Mango the snapper opens her mouth — and something in there has eyes.
2. **Rewind.** Roly leaves his mother's belly pouch as a speck, one of four hundred, and swims
   until he finds a snapper. He goes in through the **gills**. Every one of them starts as a male.
3. **The change.** He grows on the gill arches — and he is not alone. **Two** of them found Mango, both
   boys. Roly gets big enough first and becomes a *she*. The other one stays a boy, back on the gills.
   She is the one who moves forward into the mouth. (Brusca 1981: one of each sex per fish, never more —
   so this is a pair, not a crowd. See `facts.md` #7a.)
4. **The swap.** Why a parasite can do this job: a fish's "tongue" is not our kind of tongue. It is a
   bony pad with no muscle of its own, there to press food against the teeth in the roof of the mouth.
   Roly grips the base, the blood stops reaching it, and the tongue quietly fades away.
   She holds the stub with seven pairs of hooked feet and takes its place.
5. **The payoff + the cycle.** Mango keeps eating. We know, because when scientists found fish like her,
   the teeth in the roof of the mouth had left scratches on the parasite's back — Mango had been
   pressing her food against Roly. Her pouch fills with more than four hundred eggs — and the father is the
   other one who found Mango, still living back on her gills. The eggs drift off to find the next snapper.

## Tone (owner decisions, 2026-10-28)
- **Gentle fade** for the tongue: no blood, no wound. Pale watercolour, thinning, gone.
- **The sex change is a real beat**, with its own visual — not a throwaway line.
- **Roly's point of view** throughout. She is the star, not the monster.
- **The fish-tongue fact goes in** (owner), placed as the *reason* the swap works.
- **The mate gets one casual line** (owner), approved wording, corrected to the singular after
  Brusca 1981 (`facts.md` #7a: one male and one female per fish, never more):
  > "Under her belly, a pouch. More than four hundred eggs. | And the father? The other one who found
  > Mango. Still back on the gills."
  Never where it happens, and no mating scene — see `facts.md`. The second arrival is planted in beat 3
  so the line costs nothing when it lands. He should be visible on the gills in the same shot as the
  pouch, so the sound-off viewer gets it too.

## Honest ending
Nobody knows whether this is bad for Mango. The scientists' own framing: a fish with an isopod
tongue probably feeds better than a fish with no tongue and nothing in its place.

## Two misnomers worth correcting on screen
- It is **not a louse** — it is an isopod crustacean, a relative of the woodlouse and the shrimp.
- It does **not eat the tongue** — it cuts off the tongue's blood supply, and the tongue goes by itself.
