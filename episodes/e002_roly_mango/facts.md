## Hedged in the script — do not state as fact
- **What triggers the sex change.** The pattern (male → female, the first to mature claims the mouth) is
  established. The *cause* is not: the leading idea is that an established female releases a pheromone that
  stops other males changing, but this is hypothesis built on morphology. Say "scientists think" or say nothing.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymothoa_exigua · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8181168/
- **Where the pair mates — resolved enough, but still skipped.** Brusca 1981 records males on the gills
  except when "apparently 'in copula', venter to venter, with the female, in the mouth", and says this
  lineage copulates in the buccal cavity or, less often, the gill chamber. So the male comes to her. The
  script still does not show or state it (owner, 2026-10-28) — it says only **who** the mate is: the other
  one who found Mango, the male who lives on her gills. Because he lives on the gills, "back on the gills"
  is a safe way to place him; "he never left the gills" would not be.
- **How many of them are on one fish — corrected.** Popular sources imply a crowd of males on the gills.
  Brusca 1981 is explicit that it is **one of each sex** and that no multiple infestation has ever been
  recorded (#7a). The script must say **two**, not "several", and the mate is **the** other one, not
  "one of the boys".
- **Whether the big female in the mouth is still feeding.** Brusca & Gilligan: "The source of nutritional
  energy for the large, mouth-inhabiting females is unknown," and some cymothoids may be non-feeding
  commensals rather than parasites. So the blood-drinking claim belongs to the *tongue-destroying* phase only;
  never narrate her as drinking blood for the rest of her life.
- **Whether it harms the fish.** The 1983 fish were healthy, but other work reports poorer condition in fish
  carrying multiple gill isopods, especially under stress. Wikipedia notes fish with two or more parasites are
  usually underweight. The honest ending is the authors' own hypothesis (#14), framed as theirs.
- **Lifespan.** "Believed to be up to three years" (Wikipedia) — too soft to narrate. Left out.

- **Missing the last leg is not by itself proof of a manca.** In a few isopod groups the condition carries into
  adulthood. It is a safe cue for *this* animal's juvenile, not a universal rule — don't narrate it as one.

## Deliberately corrected misnomers (the script says so out loud)
- **Not a louse.** True lice are insects; this is an isopod crustacean. (#1)
- **Does not eat the tongue.** It takes the tongue's blood supply and the tongue degenerates on its own —
  no chewing. (#8) The common name is the thing that is wrong, not our drawing.

## Numbers *not* claimed
How long the tongue takes to degenerate; how many broods a female raises; how long a manca can survive
before finding a host; infestation rates anywhere outside the 1983 Guaymas sample (2 of 37).
