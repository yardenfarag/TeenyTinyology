# L001 — The shapes of bacteria (pilot)

Working title: **"Bacteria come in more shapes than you think"**. About 5–6 minutes.

## Objective
After this lesson you can:
- name the main shapes of bacteria and how their cells group together;
- read a bacterium's name for clues about its shape (*Staphylococcus* = "grape-bunch berry");
- give two reasons why shape matters to a bacterium.

**Lesson type:** categories (examples, borderline cases, one non-example), ending with a short mechanism step
("why shape matters").

## Steps
| # | Question it answers | What the viewer learns |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How different can one cell look? | Hook: one cell, many shapes; most are 1–5 µm, so for a long time shape was the first clue to telling them apart |
| 2 | What are the basic shapes? | **cocci** (spheres, Greek "berry"), **bacilli** (rods, Latin "little rod"), **spirals** |
| 3 | What happens when cocci divide and stay together? | pairs **diplococci**, chains **streptococci**, grape-like clusters **staphylococci**, squares of four **tetrads**, cubes of eight **sarcinae**. Name decoding: *Staphylococcus aureus* = "golden grape-bunch berry"; *Streptococcus* = "chain of berries" |
| 4 | And rods? | single rods, chains **streptobacilli**, side-by-side **palisades** (*Corynebacterium*). Borderline: the short, plump **coccobacillus** (*Haemophilus*), "a rod that can't decide" |
| 5 | How many ways can a cell curve? | **vibrio** (a comma: *Vibrio cholerae*), **spirillum** (a stiff spiral), **spirochete** (a flexible corkscrew with its flagella inside the cell: *Treponema*, *Borrelia*) |
| 6 | Are there stranger ones? | branching threads (*Streptomyces*, which gives soil its smell after rain), stars (*Stella*), cells with a stalk (*Caulobacter*), no fixed shape at all (*Mycoplasma*, which has no cell wall), and giants you can see without a microscope (*Thiomargarita*). Non-example: the square cell *Haloquadratum* looks like a bacterium but is an archaeon, so it gets a red "not a bacterium!" |
| 7 | Why does shape matter? | the cell wall holds the shape (no wall, no fixed shape → *Mycoplasma*); shape changes surface area for feeding; a corkscrew can drill through thick fluids like mucus. Hedge where the science is still open |
| — | Wrap | zoom out to the whole board as a map; the outro logo over the faded board (the Gram-stain teaser was cut by the owner, 2026-10-27) |

- Each region gets a section title (`b.title`) in navy with a gold underline, then marker drawings with labels
  beside them. Colours: green for cocci, blue for rods, red for curves, gold for highlights; the odd ones get mixed
  colours; the "not a bacterium" cross in red.
- **Camera route:** A → B → C (pan right), C → D (straight down), D → E → F (pan left), then zoom out to the
  whole board. Every move goes between neighbouring regions, so the frame always holds content.
- **Eraser:** the logo is wiped off region A before the hook is written there.
- **Humour (dark and dry, owner 2026-10-26; the "unimaginative names" joke is cut):** grim facts said calmly,
  each sourced in `facts.md`. Candidates:
  - step 3: "golden grape-bunch berry" sounds delicious; it's also a leading cause of skin infections.
  - step 4: the coccobacillus "that couldn't decide what shape to be" did decide to cause the Black Death
    (*Yersinia pestis*); *Haemophilus influenzae*, named after a disease it doesn't cause.
  - step 5: the comma that "has ended a great many sentences" (*Vibrio cholerae*).
  - step 2 or 7: a rod makes the deadliest toxin known, "which people now pay to have injected into their faces"
    (*Clostridium botulinum*, Botox).
  - step 6: the giant bacterium, the one cell big enough to not need a microscope.
  The script will use the strongest three or four, not all of them.
