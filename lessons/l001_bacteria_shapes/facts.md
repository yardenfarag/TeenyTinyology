# L001 facts — the shapes of bacteria
Checked 2026-10-26. Every claim the narration or board makes, with sources. Wording in **bold** is safe for the
narrator to say as written. Nothing here may be stretched further than the wording given.

## A. Basics

- **"Most bacteria are about one micrometre across and a few micrometres long — far too small to see without a
  microscope."** — corrected. The plan's "0.5–5 µm long" is close but loose. Textbook figures: an average coccus is
  0.5–1.0 µm in diameter; an average bacillus is 0.5–1.0 µm wide by 1.0–4.0 µm long; spirals 1 µm to over 100 µm
  long. OpenStax: "Bacterial cells are typically about 1 µm", and an object must be about 100 µm to be visible
  without a microscope. Safe range to say: **"roughly half a micrometre to a few micrometres."** Do not say
  "0.5–5 µm" as if it were a textbook range.
  Sources: https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria, https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/1-3-types-of-microorganisms

- **"Shape is still the first thing a lab looks at: a stained smear under the microscope, and the shape and grouping
  of the cells is the first clue to what it is."** — confirmed for present-day use; the historical half ("one of the
  first ways to tell bacteria apart") is plausible but I found no textbook sentence stating it, so phrase it softly
  ("long before DNA sequencing, the microscope was all there was"). Documented current use: CDC Pink Book —
  "the appearance of lancet-shaped diplococci on Gram stain suggests pneumococcal infection"; StatPearls on
  *Haemophilus influenzae* — Gram staining is the first test and typically shows pleomorphic gram-negative
  coccobacilli. OpenStax also notes morphology "is maintained by the cell wall in combination with cytoskeletal
  elements". Caveat worth keeping: morphology alone is suggestive, never proof.
  Sources: https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-17-pneumococcal-disease.html, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK562176/, https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-3-unique-characteristics-of-prokaryotic-cells

- **"Three basic shapes: the coccus, a sphere; the bacillus, a rod; and the spirals."** — confirmed. LibreTexts
  (Kaiser) gives exactly these three basic shapes; OpenStax lists coccus (round/spherical), bacillus (rod), vibrio
  (curved rod), spirillum (long spiral) and spirochete (long loose helical spiral).
  Sources: https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria, https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-3-unique-characteristics-of-prokaryotic-cells

- **"Coccus comes from the Greek *kokkos* — a grain or seed, the word also used for a berry."** — confirmed.
  LPSN: "Gr. masc. n. kokkos, grain, seed"; CDC's *Etymologia* glosses *kokkos* as "berry". Either gloss is safe;
  "grain or seed" is the stricter one.
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/staphylococcus, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3810938/

- **"Bacillus is Latin for a little rod — a small staff or wand."** — confirmed for the meaning. LPSN:
  "L. masc. n. Bacillus, a small staff, a wand, a rod". The further step "diminutive of *baculum*" is standard in
  dictionaries but I could not open a quotable reliable source for it — do not say "diminutive of baculum" on air.
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/bacillus

- **"Spirillum is Greek for a little spiral."** — corrected. The plan said "Latin *spira*"; LPSN gives
  "Gr. fem. n. speîra, a spiral; N.L. neut. dim. n. Spirillum, a small spiral" — the root is **Greek** *speira*,
  with a New Latin diminutive ending.
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/spirillum

## B. Arrangements — cocci that divide and stay together

- **"Divide in one plane and stay joined, and you get pairs: diplococci. *Neisseria* does this — the species that
  cause gonorrhoea and meningitis — and so does *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, whose pairs are pointed like little
  lances."** — confirmed. Kaiser lists *S. pneumoniae* and *Neisseria* as the diplococcus examples; CDC's
  *Etymologia* describes *Neisseria* as a non-motile diplococcus, with only *N. gonorrhoeae* and *N. meningitidis*
  infecting humans; StatPearls and CDC describe *S. pneumoniae* as lancet-shaped, in pairs or short chains.
  "Diplo-" is from Greek *diploos* "twofold, double" (etymonline).
  Sources: https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/22/6/et-2206_article, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK470537/, https://www.etymonline.com/word/diplo-

- **"Keep dividing in that same plane and the pairs become chains: streptococci. *Streptococcus* is from Greek
  *streptos* — pliant, easily twisted — plus *kokkos*; they were named for the chains."** — corrected. The plan's
  "*streptos* = twisted/chain" needs care: LPSN glosses *streptos* as **"pliant"**, CDC's *Etymologia* glosses it
  simply as **"chain"**, and general dictionaries give "twisted". Safe narrator wording: **"*streptos* means pliant
  or twisted — like a chain"**, then **"so *Streptococcus* is the chain-forming berry."** Examples: *S. pyogenes*,
  *Enterococcus* (Kaiser). Billroth named the genus in 1874 after seeing organisms singly, in pairs, "sometimes in
  chains".
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/streptococcus, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/22/11/et-2211_article, https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria

- **"Divide in random planes and the cells pile up like a tiny bunch of grapes: staphylococci. *Staphyle* is Greek
  for a bunch of grapes. And *aureus* is Latin for golden — so *Staphylococcus aureus* is, more or less, the golden
  grape-bunch berry."** — confirmed. LPSN: "Gr. fem. n. staphylê, bunch of grapes … Staphylococcus, the grape-like
  coccus"; *aureus*: "L. masc. adj. aureus, golden". CDC *Etymologia*: Ogston (1880) wrote that "the masses looked
  like bunches of grapes"; Rosenbach (1884) named *S. aureus* for its gold colonies. Kaiser: random planes → "cocci
  arranged in irregular, often grape-like clusters", example *S. aureus*.
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/staphylococcus, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/species/staphylococcus-aureus, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3810938/

- **"The grouping is just bookkeeping of how the cell divided: one plane gives pairs and chains, two planes at right
  angles give a square of four, three planes give a cube of eight, and random planes give a cluster."** — confirmed.
  Kaiser sets out exactly this (one plane → diplococci/streptococci; two planes → tetrads; three planes → sarcinae;
  random → staphylococci); OpenStax: groupings occur "depending on the plane of cell division".
  Sources: https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria, https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-3-unique-characteristics-of-prokaryotic-cells

- **"A square of four is a tetrad — *Micrococcus luteus* does that. A cube of eight is a sarcina, Latin for a bundle
  or package; *Sarcina ventriculi* is the classic one, found in the stomach."** — confirmed, with two cautions.
  Kaiser names *Micrococcus luteus* for tetrads and gives no example for sarcinae. LPSN: "L. fem. n. Sarcina, a
  package, bundle", with *Sarcina ventriculi* a listed species. A clinical microbiology photo-quiz answer describes
  *Sarcina* dividing "in three planes, perpendicular to one another … often in tetrads but occasionally in cubes of
  eight" — i.e. real packets are often fours, not always eights. Cautions: (1) *Pediococcus* is a poor tetrad
  example — LPSN glosses the name as "coccus growing in one plane"; use *Micrococcus*. (2) *Sarcina ventriculi* has
  been proposed for reclassification as *Clostridium ventriculi*; say "*Sarcina*" without claiming the name is
  settled.
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/sarcina, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/pediococcus, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846497, https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria

## C. Rods

- **"Rods do it too. Rods in chains are streptobacilli — *Streptobacillus moniliformis*, the rat-bite fever
  bacterium, even carries the chain in its name."** — confirmed for the term and the name; corrected on the
  example. Kaiser defines streptobacillus as "bacilli arranged in chains" and names no species. CDC describes
  *S. moniliformis* as pleomorphic rods forming "tangled filaments with bulbous swellings" — a good name-decoding
  example, but do not claim neat bead-chains on a board without showing filaments. I could not verify a textbook
  statement that *Bacillus anthracis* forms chains — cut that example.
  Sources: https://www.cdc.gov/rat-bite-fever/health-care-workers/, https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria

- **"*Corynebacterium* snaps rather than splits when it divides, so the rods end up side by side like a fence —
  palisades — or at angles, in V and Y shapes that people describe as looking like Chinese letters."** — mostly
  confirmed, one correction. University of Maryland teaching page: cells "lie parallel to one another (palisades) or
  at acute angles", "in short chains ('V' or 'Y' configurations)", "clumps resembling 'Chinese letters'", caused by
  "their snapping type of division". OpenStax describes palisades as "pairs of rod-shaped cells resembling the
  letter V" and does not use "Chinese letters". **Say V and Y, not V and L** (the plan's "L" is not in either
  source). *C. diphtheriae* is the standard species.
  Sources: https://science.umd.edu/classroom/bsci424/PathogenDescriptions/Corynebacterium.htm, https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/4-4-gram-positive-bacteria

- **"Between the sphere and the rod sits the coccobacillus: a rod so short and plump it can't decide. *Haemophilus
  influenzae* is one — 0.3 to 1 micrometre, described in the lab as a pleomorphic coccobacillus — and so is
  *Yersinia pestis*."** — confirmed. StatPearls: *H. influenzae* is "a small … pleomorphic … gram-negative
  coccobacillus", typically 0.3–1 µm; CDC Pink Book calls it "a fastidious, pleomorphic, gram-negative
  coccobacillus". A PMC review describes *Y. pestis* as "a nonmotile, nonsporulated, aerobic Gram-negative bacillus
  or coccobacillus". Kaiser defines coccobacillus as "oval and similar to a coccus".
  Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK562176/, https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-8-haemophilus-influenzae.html, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7920731/

- **"*Haemophilus influenzae* got its name by mistake. Richard Pfeiffer described it in 1892 and thought it caused
  influenza. It doesn't — influenza is caused by a virus, which wasn't established until 1933. The bacterium kept
  the wrong name."** — confirmed. CDC Pink Book: "first described by Richard Pfeiffer in 1892"; Pfeiffer proposed it
  as the cause of influenza; "It was not until 1933 that it was established that influenza was caused by a virus",
  the bacterium being a cause of secondary infection. The 1933 isolation was by Smith, Andrewes and Laidlaw
  (*Lancet*). The genus name, given in 1920, means "blood-loving" (LPSN: haîma, blood + -philus, loving).
  Sources: https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-8-haemophilus-influenzae.html, https://www.aai.org/About/History/History-Articles/The-1918–1919-Influenza-Pandemic-as-Covered-in-(1), https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/haemophilus

- **"*Yersinia pestis* causes plague. In the Black Death, from 1347 to 1351, something like a third of Europe died —
  estimates run from a quarter to a half."** — corrected. The plan's "30–50%+" is at the high end of the literature.
  A PMC plague review: the second pandemic "killed an estimated one-third of the European population". A PMC history
  review: "at least one third of the European population (more than 25 million people) died between 1347 and 1352".
  The PNAS 2000 paper: "17–28 million Europeans (from 30 to 40% of the total population) between the years 1347 and
  1351". CDC's EID journal is more conservative still (a quarter to a third). Safest narrator wording: **"roughly a
  third of Europe — estimates range from a quarter to a half."** SUPERSEDED: see the fact audit at the end of this file
  ("mid-1300s", "at least a third"; Wikipedia gives 1346–1353 and 30–60%).
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7920731/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7513766/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC18844/

## D. Curves

- **"A vibrio is a curved rod, a comma. *Vibrio cholerae* causes cholera, and the name is about movement: Latin
  *vibro*, to move to and fro, to vibrate — 'that which vibrates'."** — confirmed. Kaiser: vibrio = "a curved or
  comma-shaped rod", example *V. cholerae*. LPSN: "L. v. vibro, to set in tremulous motion, move to and fro,
  vibrate; N.L. masc. n. Vibrio, that which vibrates" — "the vibrating, darting organism". ("Cholera" itself is from
  Greek *cholē*, bile — CDC *Etymologia*.)
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/vibrio, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/17/11/et-1711_article, https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria

- **"Cholera is still with us: the WHO estimates 1.3 to 4 million cases and 21,000 to 143,000 deaths a year. Seven
  pandemics since 1817 — the seventh began in 1961 and has never ended."** — confirmed. WHO fact sheet gives
  "1.3 to 4.0 million cases and 21 000 to 143 000 deaths … each year" and the current pandemic beginning in south
  Asia in 1961; CDC repeats the same burden figures. CDC's EID: "Global pandemic spread of cholera from its
  ancestral home in Bengal was first documented in 1817", with six further pandemics and "we are currently in the
  throes of the seventh pandemic".
  Sources: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cholera, https://www.cdc.gov/cholera/about/index.html, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/17/11/11-1109_article

- **"A spirillum is a thick, stiff spiral that swims with tufts of flagella on the outside, at each end.
  *Spirillum volutans* is the classic one. *Campylobacter* — Greek for 'curved rod' — is the everyday curved
  example."** — confirmed, with one thing to avoid. Kaiser: spirillum = "a thick, rigid spiral"; *Spirillum
  volutans* studies describe a helical cell driven by a polar flagellar bundle at each pole. CDC *Etymologia*:
  *Campylobacter* from *kampylos* (curved) + *baktron* (rod), "slender, curved bacilli". **Do not say the rigid
  spiral shape is what makes it swim** — non-helical mutants of *S. volutans* swim as fast as helical cells, and the
  authors conclude helical shape is not required for motility in that species.
  Sources: https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/19/8/et-1908_article, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC221807/

- **"A spirochete is a thin, flexible corkscrew, and its flagella are *inside* the cell — in the periplasm, between
  the outer membrane and the cell body. *Treponema pallidum* causes syphilis; *Borrelia burgdorferi*, carried by
  ticks, causes Lyme disease; *Leptospira* curls its ends into hooks."** — confirmed. Kaiser: spirochete = "a thin,
  flexible spiral", examples *Leptospira* and *Treponema pallidum*. A 2020 review: spirochete flagella are "hidden
  within the periplasmic space" (hence periplasmic flagella); *B. burgdorferi* is introduced by a tick bite;
  *Leptospira* cell ends form a left-handed spiral or hook. CDC *Etymologia*: *Treponema* = *trepo* (to turn) +
  *nema* (thread), spiral-shaped, flagella producing a wave-like motion that "helps them invade tissue".
  *Leptospira* = *leptos* (slender) + *speira* (coil); Stimson first called it *Spirochaeta interrogans* because it
  looked like a question mark.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7225975/, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/27/4/et-2704_article, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/19/3/et-1903_article

- **"Spirochetes are at their best in thick, gel-like stuff: mucus-like gels speed them up, where open water does
  not."** — corrected / needs hedging. The plan's "move through viscous media better than many other bacteria" is
  too strong. The 2020 review: motility is improved by gel-like polymer solutions (methylcellulose, PVP, mucin),
  which "may help them accumulate in the mucus layer in vivo" — but in plain liquid spirochetes are *slower* than
  externally flagellated bacteria (*E. coli*/*Salmonella* 20–30 µm/s; fastest spirochete ~15 µm/s; *T. pallidum*
  ~2 µm/s). So: **"in a thick gel they do better than you'd expect — in open water they're slowpokes."**
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7225975/, https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/microbiolspec.psib-0030-2019

## E. The odd ones

- **"*Streptomyces* grows as branching threads — a mycelium, like a tiny fungus. It makes geosmin, the compound
  behind the earthy smell of soil, strongest after rain or when you dig; people can smell it at about a hundred
  parts per trillion. And it is one of our great antibiotic sources: more than two-thirds of the clinically useful
  antibiotics of natural origin, streptomycin among them."** — confirmed. PMC review: *Streptomyces* are
  filamentous, forming "branching substrate and aerial mycelia", produce geosmin, linked to the "wet earth odor" of
  soil; actinomycetes make "two-thirds of all microbial antibiotics" with ~80% of those from *Streptomyces*.
  OpenStax: a "fungus-like (filamentous) bacillus" linked to soil's earthy smell, producing "more than two-thirds of
  clinically useful antibiotics"; streptomycin came from *S. griseus*. John Innes Centre: geosmin gives "the
  evocative, earthy smell", most noticeable "after recent rainfall or while digging", detectable at ~100 parts per
  trillion, and every *Streptomyces* species studied makes it.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9558229/, https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/4-4-gram-positive-bacteria, https://www.jic.ac.uk/press-release/research-unearths-the-science-behind-the-smell-of-spring/

- **"There are star-shaped bacteria: flat, six-pronged stars, from soil and fresh water."** — confirmed; the name
  needs care. LPSN: "L. fem. n. Stella, a star, to denote star-shaped morphology of cells"; species *Stella humosa*
  (soil, Krasnodar) and *Stella vacuolata*. A 2019 genome paper describes them as "flat, six-pronged, star-shaped
  bacteria", "found in various environments, e.g., freshwater, soil, and sewage". **Correction: as of 2025 the name
  *Stella* is illegitimate and the correct genus is *Allostella* ("another Stella"), so write the board label as
  "*Stella*, now *Allostella*" or just "star-shaped bacteria".**
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/stella, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/allostella, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6687932

- **"*Caulobacter crescentus* is a curved rod — its curve comes from a protein called crescentin — that grows a
  stalk with glue on the end. When it divides, one daughter swims away with a flagellum and the other stays
  cemented in place."** — confirmed. Review: "curved rod morphology"; "creS mutant cells grow as straight rods";
  "a slender, polar extension of the cell body called the stalk"; "an adhesive polysaccharide-rich matrix (holdfast)
  is secreted at the end of the stalk"; division gives "a motile daughter or 'swarmer' cell that possesses a polar
  flagellum" and "a sessile daughter or 'stalked' cell". LPSN: *Caulobacter* = *caulis* (stalk) + *bacter* (rod).
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554946/, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/caulobacter

- **"That glue is one of the strongest natural adhesives known — around 70 newtons per square millimetre, roughly
  three times commercial superglue."** — uncertain, hedge as "one of the strongest known", not "the strongest".
  The figures come from press coverage of the 2006 PNAS study (Nature News: "around 70 newtons per square
  millimetre", superglue "gives up at about 25 newtons per square millimetre", subtitle "three times stronger than
  superglue"); the "strongest biological adhesion ever discovered" line is a researcher quote in a press release,
  not a measured ranking. I could not open the PNAS paper itself.
  Sources: https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060410/full/news060410-1.html

- **"*Mycoplasma* has no cell wall at all, so it has no fixed shape — pleomorphic, the textbooks say. They are among
  the smallest self-replicating cells known, and *M. pneumoniae* causes the mild pneumonia people call walking
  pneumonia."** — confirmed. OpenStax: "Lacking cell walls, these very small cells can take on many shapes",
  "pleomorphic", *M. pneumoniae* causes a mild pneumonia "sometimes called 'walking pneumonia'". Reviews call
  mycoplasmas "the tiniest self-replicating prokaryotes", "devoid of a cell wall", passing through bacteria-retaining
  filters. CDC explains the nickname: people with this mild pneumonia often stay on their feet.
  Sources: https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/4-4-gram-positive-bacteria, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11097136, https://www.cdc.gov/mycoplasma/about/index.html

- **"And there are giants. *Thiomargarita* means 'sulfur pearl': *Thiomargarita namibiensis* is 0.1 to 0.75
  millimetres across — already visible if you look closely. In 2022 researchers described *Thiomargarita
  magnifica* from Caribbean mangroves: single cells averaging over 9 millimetres long, about the size of a human
  eyelash, visible to the naked eye."** — confirmed, with corrections. Kaiser: *T. namibiensis* is 100–750 µm in
  diameter. LPSN: *Thiomargarita* = *theîon* (sulfur) + *margarita* (pearl). The 2022 *Science* paper (Volland et
  al., 376:1453–1458): "an average cell length greater than 9,000 µm", "visible to the naked eye", against ~2 µm for
  most bacteria and ~750 µm for the previous largest; measured filaments ran "up to 9.66 mm long"; found in
  Guadeloupe mangroves. **Corrections: (1) "up to ~2 cm" is a press quote, not a published measurement — say
  "about a centimetre" or "up to roughly 1 cm". (2) The eyelash comparison is from AP/press coverage, not the paper —
  fine as a size image, not as a citation. (3) The name is *Candidatus* Thiomargarita magnifica and is not validly
  published, so avoid "officially named".**
  Sources: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.02.16.480423v1.abstract, https://www.sci.news/biology/thiomargarita-magnifica-10936.html, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/thiomargarita, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/species/thiomargarita-magnifica, https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Kaiser)/Unit_1%3A_Introduction_to_Microbiology_and_Prokaryotic_Cell_Anatomy/2%3A_The_Prokaryotic_Cell_-_Bacteria/2.1%3A_Sizes_Shapes_and_Arrangements_of_Bacteria

- **"One shape is a trap. *Haloquadratum walsbyi* is a flat square cell from salty water — and it is not a
  bacterium at all. It's an archaeon."** — confirmed. LPSN gives the full lineage Archaea → Methanobacteriati →
  Methanobacteriota → Halobacteria → Halobacteriales → Haloferacaceae → *Haloquadratum*, and the etymology
  "salt square" (*hals*, salt + *quadratum*, square). The habitat wording should stay general — **"salty water,
  salt ponds and brines"** — I verified the domain and the name, not a specific isolation site.
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/haloquadratum

## F. Why shape matters

- **"The cell wall — a mesh of peptidoglycan — is what holds the shape. Take the wall away and cells go round or
  irregular: that's what L-forms do, and it's why *Mycoplasma*, which never had a wall, has no fixed shape. Inside,
  proteins help set the form: MreB keeps rods the right width, and crescentin gives *Caulobacter* its curve."**
  — confirmed. OpenStax: "morphology is maintained by the cell wall in combination with cytoskeletal elements".
  L-form review: under stress bacteria "may shed their cell walls, forming bacterial L-forms", which can be "round
  or irregularly shaped"; the wall "aids in protecting bacterial cells against hypoosmotic conditions, maintaining
  their morphology". *Caulobacter* review: "creS mutant cells grow as straight rods"; "Uniform cell width … is
  maintained by MreB", and MreB-depleted cells "widen, become lemon-shaped, and eventually lyse".
  Sources: https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-3-unique-characteristics-of-prokaryotic-cells, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13280103/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554946/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1594593

- **"Size and shape change how much surface a cell has for its volume, and bacteria feed by diffusion across that
  surface — which is a big reason they stay small. Growing into a long thread adds feeding surface too."**
  — corrected. Young's review is careful here: "reliance on diffusion creates the strong tendency to form smaller
  cells", and "elongating increases a cell's uptake-proficient surface without changing its surface-to-volume ratio
  appreciably". So the plan's "thin rods/filaments have more surface for their volume than spheres of the same
  volume" is geometrically true of a *thin* rod but is **not** the textbook explanation — **lead with "small cells
  have more surface per volume", and say filaments add surface, not ratio.**
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1594593

- **"A spiral body seems to help in thick, gluey places. *Helicobacter pylori* lives in stomach mucus, and the
  helical cells swim measurably faster there than straight mutants — about a tenth to a fifth faster. Scientists
  think the shape helps; what it isn't is a drill."** — corrected. The corkscrew-drilling image is explicitly
  rejected by the people who tested it: "the helical shape of *H. pylori* does not help it bore its way like a
  corkscrew through the gel-like mucus layer". Measured effects: straight-rod mutants showed "7–21% reduction in
  speed and a lower fraction of motile bacteria", and in gastric mucin "an 8–13% increase in speed of the helical
  bacteria compared to straight rods"; a review calls these effects "subtle" while concluding helical shape "does
  appear to fit the criteria of a virulence factor". **The board must not say "drills through mucus".**
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4857613/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7247928/

- **"Scientists think shape also helps with sticking to surfaces, with not being eaten, and with being carried
  around. A long filament is harder for a hungry protist to swallow."** — confirmed as a hedged claim. Young's 2006
  review lists eight selective factors for shape — nutrient access, division and segregation, attachment, passive
  dispersal, active motility, polar differentiation, predator escape and differentiation — and flags several of the
  arguments as untested ("The effects of alternate diffusion barriers are hypothetical, however"; "The idea has not
  been tested"). Grazing evidence: cells "<7 µm were highly vulnerable to grazers, whereas filamentous cells were
  resistant to grazing"; some bacteria filament *in response* to grazers. Counter-evidence exists — a 2004 study
  found a nanoflagellate that eats filamentous strains and "challenges the current assumption that filamentous
  bacteria are protected from grazing". So say **"scientists think"** and keep it to one line.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1594593, https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/aem.65.1.25-35.1999, https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/aem.70.1.332-339.2004

## G. Dark-humour lines

- **"*Clostridium botulinum* is a rod. It makes botulinum toxin — the most lethal biological toxin known. Diluted
  enormously, that same toxin is a medicine and a cosmetic: Botox. People pay to have it injected into their
  faces."** — confirmed. OpenStax: *C. botulinum* "produces botulinum neurotoxin, the most lethal biological toxin
  known", blocking acetylcholine release and causing flaccid paralysis. A 2025 clinical review describes it as "a
  neurotoxin produced by the anaerobic rod-shaped bacterium Clostridium botulinum", FDA-approved from December 1989
  (strabismus, blepharospasm), with BOTOX Cosmetic approved for glabellar frown lines in April 2002 and lateral
  canthal lines ("crow's feet") in 2013; effects appear in ~2 weeks and last ~3–4 months.
  Sources: https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/4-4-gram-positive-bacteria, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11943293/

- **"*Bacillus anthracis*, the rod that causes anthrax, forms spores, and the bacteria live naturally in soil around
  the world."** — partly confirmed; **cut the "survive for decades" figure.** OpenStax: "*B. anthracis* is the
  pathogen that causes anthrax" and the genus forms endospores. CDC: "The bacteria are found naturally in soil
  around the world", and refers to spores. I did not verify any persistence figure, so the narration should stop at
  "spores, and the bacteria live in soil" — no numbers, no durability claim.
  Sources: https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/4-4-gram-positive-bacteria, https://www.cdc.gov/anthrax/about/index.html, https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/20/9/et-2009_article

- **"*Staphylococcus aureus* — the grape-bunch one — is a common cause of skin infections, and it lives quietly on
  the skin or up the nose of roughly a quarter to a third of people."** — confirmed with the range. CDC
  *Etymologia* associates the genus with "surgical and skin infections, respiratory disease, and food poisoning".
  Carriage: reviews give "approximately 20–30% of carriers in the general population" for nasal carriage, and a
  CDC-hosted study cites NHANES 2003–2004 at 28.6%. **Say "roughly a quarter to a third", or "about 30% of people",
  not "30%" as a precise number** (throat-only carriers push totals higher in some studies).
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3810938/, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6186810/, https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/194727/cdc_194727_DS1.pdf

- **"*Streptococcus pyogenes* — group A strep, the chain one — gives you strep throat. Rarely, the same bacterium
  causes necrotizing fasciitis, the flesh-eating infection."** — confirmed. CDC: group A strep causes "strep throat,
  scarlet fever, necrotizing fasciitis, and more"; necrotizing fasciitis is "a rare, but serious bacterial
  infection" and group A strep is its most common cause; up to 1 in 5 people die from it even with treatment.
  Keep "rarely" in the sentence.
  Sources: https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/about/index.html, https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/about/necrotizing-fasciitis.html

## Watch-outs

1. **Size claim.** Drop "0.5–5 µm long". Say "about half a micrometre to a few micrometres". Nothing in the
   textbooks supports a tidy 0.5–5 range.
2. **"Shape was one of the first ways to tell bacteria apart."** Not sourced as a historical statement; soften to
   "long before DNA, the microscope was all there was". Current diagnostic use *is* sourced (Gram stain).
3. ***streptos*.** Sources disagree: LPSN "pliant", CDC "chain", dictionaries "twisted". Say "pliant or twisted —
   like a chain"; don't present a single gloss as the etymology.
4. ***spirillum* is Greek, not Latin** (*speîra*). Fix the plan.
5. **"Diminutive of *baculum*"** — unverified; just say "Latin for a little rod".
6. **Corynebacterium: V and Y, not V and L.** "Chinese letters" is standard on teaching pages but absent from
   OpenStax — attribute it as "what microbiologists call…".
7. **Streptobacilli example:** use *Streptobacillus moniliformis* (named for the chain), not *Bacillus anthracis*
   chains, which I could not verify. Note CDC describes *S. moniliformis* as tangled filaments, so draw it loosely.
8. **Tetrads:** use *Micrococcus luteus*. *Pediococcus* is a bad example (LPSN glosses the name as growing in one
   plane). Sarcinae: real *Sarcina* packets are "often tetrads, occasionally cubes of eight" — say "up to a cube of
   eight". Also *Sarcina ventriculi* has a proposed reclassification (*Clostridium ventriculi*).
9. ***Stella* has been renamed *Allostella* (2025)** and the old name is illegitimate. Label the board "star-shaped
   bacteria (*Stella*, now *Allostella*)".
10. ***Thiomargarita magnifica*:** "up to 2 cm" and the eyelash comparison are press, not paper. The paper says
    average cell length >9,000 µm (~0.9 cm), filaments measured up to 9.66 mm. Say "about a centimetre". It is
    *Candidatus* — name not validly published.
11. **Caulobacter holdfast:** "one of the strongest natural glues known", with the ~70 N/mm² / ~3× superglue figures
    attributed to the 2006 study's press coverage. Not "the strongest glue in nature".
12. **No corkscrew drilling.** The *H. pylori* researchers explicitly reject the drill image. Allowed: "the helical
    cells swim about 10–20% faster in stomach mucus than straight mutants, and scientists think that helps."
13. **Spirochetes are not fast.** They beat other bacteria in gels, not in water — in open liquid *E. coli* is
    faster. Phrase it as "built for thick, gluey places".
14. **Surface-to-volume.** Lead with "small cells have more surface per volume"; filaments add feeding surface
    without changing the ratio much. Don't claim a thin rod beats a sphere "of the same volume" as if that were the
    textbook point.
15. **Spiral shape and swimming.** Don't say the spiral is what lets a spirillum swim: straight *Spirillum
    volutans* mutants swim just as fast.
16. **Black Death.** SUPERSEDED by the fact audit (end of file): say "In the mid-1300s … at least a third of Europe". Avoid
    "30–50%+".
17. **Anthrax line: cut the spore-longevity number.** Safe version: *"Bacillus anthracis* is a rod that forms
    spores, and it lives naturally in soil around the world." Nothing about how long spores last, and nothing about
    deliberate release.
18. **Botox line is safe as written** — the "most lethal biological toxin known" wording is OpenStax's, and the
    Botox turn is well documented (FDA 1989 therapeutic, 2002 cosmetic). Keep the joke on the *cosmetic* side
    ("people pay to have it injected into their faces"), with no doses, amounts or lethality comparisons.
19. **S. aureus carriage:** "roughly a quarter to a third of people", not a flat 30%.
20. **S. pyogenes:** keep the word "rarely" before necrotizing fasciitis.

## Fact audit (2026-10-27) — wording changed so it holds under every major source
Full table: `review/fact_audit.md`. Owner asked after spotting that Wikipedia dates the Black Death 1346–1353.
- Black Death: "In the mid-1300s, it killed at least a third of Europe" (Britannica ~1/3 and 1347–51; Wikipedia
  1346–1353 and 30–60%; Benedictow 2021 ~60%). Board: "mid-1300s", "at least 1/3 of Europe".
- *H. influenzae*: "first described in the 1890s" (CDC Pink Book 1892, Wikipedia 1893).
- Opening: "Almost every bacterium is a single cell" (*Streptomyces* is multicellular; the board marks it).
- Staphylococci: "split in a new direction each time" (textbooks: random planes; 2010/2020 imaging: each plane
  perpendicular to the last). Board: "changing ways".
- Cholera deaths are WHO estimates (21,000–143,000/yr): "it's thought to kill"; board "est. 20,000+ a year".
- *H. pylori*: "the mucus of the stomach" (about half of people carry it).
- Botox: "In tiny, purified doses, it's also Botox" (owner asked, 2026-10-27, whether the toxin is "highly diluted"
  or genetically engineered). It is neither: the toxin is produced by the bacterium and purified, and the medicine
  works because the dose is tiny. Avoid "diluted enormously" and any production detail.
- Star-shaped bacteria ARE named, in the voice and on the board: **Allostella** (board note "star-shaped (was
  Stella)"). Owner asked why this one had no example (2026-10-27). LPSN: *Stella* Vasilyeva 1985 is validly
  published but **illegitimate**; *Allostella* Deshmukh and Oren 2025 ("another Stella") is the correct name, with
  *A. humosa* (type) and *A. vacuolata*. Keep the old name on the board, because that is what people search for.
  https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/allostella  https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/stella
- *Thiomargarita* scale note: the board says "really thousands of times bigger", not "(not to scale!)" — the owner
  read the old note and could not tell what it meant (2026-10-27). A board note must say what it means to a viewer,
  never be a note to ourselves. ~9,000 um long against a ~1-2 um typical cell is thousands of times.
- Mycoplasma board: "no wall, no fixed shape" (some wall-less species keep a shape, e.g. M. pneumoniae's flask
  shape, Spiroplasma's spiral). Never "no shape" or "shapeless" on the board.
