# L002 facts — quorum sensing
Checked 2026-11-11. Every claim the narration or board makes, with sources. Wording in **bold** is safe for the
narrator to say as written. Nothing here may be stretched further than the wording given. Every date, number, name
and superlative below was also checked against Wikipedia (and Britannica where it has an article; Britannica's
quorum-sensing article could not be opened, HTTP 403, so only its search summary was seen).

## A. The glowing squid (step 1)

- **"This is the Hawaiian bobtail squid. It hunts at night, and on a moonlit night its body would cast a shadow on
  the sand below. So it glows from underneath, matching the moonlight, and the shadow disappears. Biologists call
  this counter-illumination."** — confirmed. ASM (2025): the light "matches the moonlight above, masking the shadow
  that would otherwise be cast on the ocean floor". Jones & Nishiguchi 2004 (*Marine Biology* 144:1151) were the
  first to test it: the squid modifies the symbionts' light as downwelling light changes. Kremer et al. 2018: the
  light is used in "the squid's nightly camouflaging behavior, or counterillumination". Wikipedia (squid) agrees:
  the light hides the silhouette "when viewed from below". "Hunts at night" is safe as "active at night" (UW news:
  "at night when the squid is most active"; Wikipedia: inactive and hidden by day).
  Sources: https://asm.org/magazine/2025/fall/shedding-light-on-the-vibrio-squid-symbiosis, https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/19916.html, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6172772/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_bobtail_squid

- **"The light isn't made by the squid. It's made by bacteria living in a special light organ: *Aliivibrio
  fischeri* — you'll often see it under its old name, *Vibrio fischeri*."** — confirmed. LPSN: *Aliivibrio
  fischeri* is the correct name; *Vibrio fischeri* (and *Photobacterium fischeri*) are synonyms; transferred by
  Urbanczyk et al. 2007. *Aliivibrio* = "the other *Vibrio*". **Wikipedia titles the article *Aliivibrio
  fischeri*** and both Wikipedia articles use *A. fischeri*; Wikipedia also notes the rename "has not been
  universally adopted". Most squid papers and ASM still say *V. fischeri* (Verma & Miyashiro explain they keep the
  *Vibrio* name because most host–microbe studies use it). L001 rule applies: say the current name, mention the old
  one. Board: "*Aliivibrio fischeri* (was *Vibrio fischeri*)".
  Sources: https://lpsn.dsmz.de/species/aliivibrio-fischeri, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/aliivibrio, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliivibrio_fischeri, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3759917/

- **"And here's the strange part: these bacteria only glow when there are lots of them. Alone in the open sea,
  they stay dark."** — confirmed. Verma & Miyashiro 2013: bioluminescence is repressed at low cell density;
  in the light organ, where densities can exceed 10^10 cells per mL, luminescence is induced by LuxI–LuxR
  signalling. O'Toole 2016: light appeared only after the growing cells had "conditioned" the medium.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3759917/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4751818/

- **"They live in a pouch on the squid's underside, called the light organ. It even has a reflector, and a lens,
  to aim their glow downwards."** — confirmed (added with script v2, owner asked what a light organ is). The light
  organ is a bilobed organ in the centre of the mantle cavity, on the ventral (under) side of the ink sac; the
  bacteria live in crypts of host tissue that open to the mantle cavity through pores; a thick reflector directs the
  light ventrally and a muscle-derived lens covers it; the ink sac acts like an iris (McFall-Ngai & Montgomery 1990;
  Science in the Classroom summary of Jones & Nishiguchi). "Pouch" is a plain-language simplification of "organ
  with crypts in the mantle cavity"; "on the underside" holds (ventral surface of the ink sac, light aimed down).
  Sources: https://bobtailsquid.ink/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/McFall-NgaiMontgomery1990.pdf, https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org/research-papers/harnessing-symbiosis-hide-plain-sight, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_bobtail_squid

- **Bridge (cut in script v2: owner, no references to the shapes lesson): "*Aliivibrio fischeri* is a cousin of *Vibrio cholerae* — the comma from the shapes lesson. Same
  family; until 2007 it was even in the same genus."** — confirmed. Wikipedia (*Aliivibrio*, *V. cholerae*)
  places both in family Vibrionaceae, order Vibrionales; LPSN gives the 2007 transfer out of *Vibrio*. "Glowing
  cousin" is fine as an image.
  Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliivibrio, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrio_cholerae, https://lpsn.dsmz.de/species/aliivibrio-fischeri

## B. Sensing a crowd (step 2)

- **"Each cell leaks a little signal molecule. When cells are few, it drifts away. When many cells are packed
  together, it builds up. Past a threshold, the genes switch on across the whole population."** — confirmed, one
  correction. Rutherford & Bassler 2012: quorum sensing is "the production, detection, and response to
  extracellular signaling molecules called autoinducers"; Wikipedia: at low density signals may "just diffuse
  away". **Correction: don't say "every cell switches on together" as if in perfect unison** — single *V. fischeri*
  cells vary considerably in how brightly they glow even when the population is induced (Verma & Miyashiro).
  "The crowd switches on" / "the population switches on" is safe.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3759917/

- **"The signal is called an autoinducer. The first clue came around 1970, from glowing marine bacteria: Kenneth
  Nealson, Terry Platt and Woodland Hastings saw that the light switched on only after the cells had conditioned
  their own broth. They called it autoinduction."** — confirmed. Nealson, Platt & Hastings, *J. Bacteriol.*
  104:313 (Oct 1970). O'Toole's 2016 *J. Bacteriol.* Classic Spotlight: they called this "autoinduction", a term
  still used. Wikipedia (Quorum sensing and Autoinducer) gives the same three names and 1970. "Around 1970" or
  "in 1970" both hold.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4751818/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoinducer

- **"The name 'quorum sensing' came later, in 1994, from Clay Fuqua, Stephen Winans and Peter Greenberg."** —
  confirmed. Fuqua, Winans & Greenberg, *J. Bacteriol.* 176:269 (1994) (PMC205046). O'Toole 2016: they
  "introduced the term ... in print in a 1994 minireview". Wikipedia credits Winans with the term, in that review
  with Fuqua and Greenberg (and lists rejected names: gridlockins, communiolins, quoromones — a possible light
  aside). Naming all three is consistent with Wikipedia. (First names are standard but were not on the pages I
  opened; surnames alone are the strict version.)
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC205046/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4751818/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing

- **"A quorum is the smallest number of members who must be present before a meeting can make decisions. The word
  is Latin — *quorum*, 'of whom' — from the old commissions that named justices of the peace."** — corrected.
  The plan's "needed for a vote to count" is close but loose: Wikipedia defines a quorum as "the minimum number of
  members of a group necessary to constitute the group at a meeting"; Dictionary.com: "required to be present to
  transact business legally". Etymology: Dictionary.com — Latin *quōrum* "of whom", "from its use in Latin legal
  formulas for commissioning justices of the peace", first recorded 1400–50; Wiktionary quotes the commission
  wording "quorum vos unum esse volumus" ("of whom we wish you to be one"); Wikipedia agrees (genitive plural of
  *qui*). Etymonline, Merriam-Webster and Collins refused automated access (403) — not opened.
  Sources: https://www.dictionary.com/browse/quorum, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/quorum, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum

## C. The circuit (step 3)

- **"LuxI is the enzyme that makes the signal — a small molecule with a fatty tail, called an AHL. In the squid's
  bacteria it's small enough to slip straight through the cell's membrane, in and out. Inside, a protein called
  LuxR waits. When enough signal has built up, LuxR grabs it and switches on the light genes — and one of those
  genes is *luxI* itself. So the more signal there is, the more signal gets made. That loop is the 'auto' in
  autoinducer."** — confirmed. Verma & Miyashiro: LuxI synthesises 3-oxo-C6-HSL; above a threshold it binds LuxR,
  which activates the *lux* operon; *luxI* is in that operon, so the signal amplifies its own synthesis. Rutherford
  & Bassler: "a feed-forward autoinduction loop that floods the vicinity with AI". Kaplan & Greenberg 1985: the
  *V. fischeri* autoinducer enters cells "by simple diffusion"; cell concentrations "equaled external
  concentrations". Wikipedia (*A. fischeri*): "The luxI gene produces the autoinducer (N-acyl homoserine
  lactone, AHL)", LuxR binds it, positive feedback; the lux signal is 3OC6-HSL. **Caveat: not every AHL diffuses
  freely** — *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*'s long-tailed 3-oxo-C12-HSL is pumped out (MexAB-OprM), while its short
  C4-HSL diffuses (Pearson et al. 1999); Wikipedia (Autoinducer) says AHLs move "by both passive transport and
  active transport". So keep "slips through the membrane" to the squid's bacteria, not to AHLs in general.
  "Fatty" is fair: AHL = acyl (fatty-acid) chain + homoserine lactone ring; Wikipedia calls them small neutral
  lipids. (*A. fischeri* also has a second AHL system, AinS/C8-HSL, and AI-2 — the board can ignore them.)
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3759917/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC219261/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC93498/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliivibrio_fischeri, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoinducer

- **"Every dawn, the squid vents most of its bacteria into the sea — around nine in ten, or more. The few left
  behind regrow within hours, and by nightfall the light organ is full and glowing again."** — confirmed,
  hedged number. Sources differ: ~95% (Kremer et al. 2018; Heath-Heckman et al. 2016; Wikipedia squid article "up
  to 95%"), ~90% (UW–Madison news; Wikipedia *A. fischeri* article "about 90%"), 90–95% (ASM 2025). **The two
  Wikipedia articles disagree with each other (90% vs up to 95%)** — so never say a single figure; "most",
  "around nine in ten or more" or "90–95%" holds under all. Regrowth: Kremer 2018 — the remaining ~5% grow
  exponentially "to reach a plateau around 6 h postdawn"; Wikipedia (*A. fischeri*): the rest regrow "before the
  next night". "Within hours" and "by nightfall" are both safe.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6172772/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5219934/, https://news.wisc.edu/in-sync-squid-glowing-companions-march-in-genetic-harmony, https://asm.org/magazine/2025/fall/shedding-light-on-the-vibrio-squid-symbiosis, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_bobtail_squid, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliivibrio_fischeri

## D. Languages (step 4)

- **"Not every bacterium speaks the same chemical language. The squid's bacteria and *Pseudomonas* use AHLs —
  small fatty molecules."** — corrected. The plan's "*Vibrio* uses AHLs" is **not fair for *Vibrio cholerae***:
  its main signals are CAI-1 and AI-2 (Wang et al. 2013; Rutherford & Bassler), and Wang et al. describe
  *V. fluvialis* as the only human pathogen then known with both AHL and CAI-1/AI-2 systems. *V. fischeri* and
  *V. harveyi* do use AHLs. Since the lesson's later *Vibrio* is *V. cholerae*, **label the board "squid bacteria,
  *Pseudomonas*: AHL"**, not "*Vibrio*: AHL". *P. aeruginosa* uses LasI/LasR and RhlI/RhlR AHL circuits
  (Wikipedia; de Kievit & Iglewski).
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3754567/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC101676/

- **"*Staphylococcus* and *Streptococcus* — the grapes and the chains — use short chains of amino acids instead:
  tiny protein fragments. These don't slip through the membrane; the cell pumps them out, and they're caught by
  receptors on the cell's surface."** — confirmed, with one nuance. Rutherford & Bassler: Gram-positive AIPs "are
  detected via membrane-bound two-component sensor kinases"; *S. aureus* uses "a canonical ... two-component QS
  system encoded by the agr locus". Wikipedia (Autoinducer): peptides "usually require specialized transport
  mechanisms" (often ABC transporters) and are detected by "a histidine sensor kinase protein of a two-component
  regulatory system". *Streptococcus pneumoniae*: the competence-stimulating peptide (CSP), exported by ComAB, binds
  the histidine kinase ComD (Shanker & Federle 2017; Wikipedia: "a peptide pheromone"). **Nuance: some streptococcal
  peptides (e.g. XIP in *S. mutans*) are imported and bind a receptor inside the cell**, and Wikipedia mentions AIPs
  binding a transcription factor in the cytosol. So say **"mostly caught by receptors on the surface"** if the
  narration generalises. No "Gram-positive/negative" on air (owner).
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoinducer, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5295010/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing

- **"And one signal, called AI-2, is made by many different kinds of bacteria. Some scientists think it may work
  as a shared word between species — but that's still debated."** — soften. LuxS (which makes AI-2) is widespread
  (Wikipedia; Rutherford & Bassler: "consistent with a role for AI-2 in interspecies communication"). Federle &
  Bassler 2003 proposed AI-2 "appears to be universal and facilitates interspecies communication". Against:
  Rezzonico & Duffy 2008 found known AI-2 receptors only in a few groups (LuxPQ "restricted to Vibrionales"), and
  "in most other species ... the role of LuxS appears to be limited to metabolism". Wikipedia reports the same
  genomic caveat (LuxP only in *Vibrio*). Keep both halves: "made by many species" (fact) + "may be a shared
  signal, debated" (hedge). Never "a universal language".
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC228483/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2561040/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing

## E. Attack, or leave (step 5)

- **"*Pseudomonas aeruginosa* uses its count to time an attack. While it's few, it holds back. Once there are
  enough, it switches on toxins and enzymes that damage tissue, and builds a biofilm — a slimy, protected city of
  cells."** — confirmed. Rutherford & Bassler: QS-controlled factors include "elastase, proteases, pyocyanin,
  lectin, swarming motility, rhamnolipids, and toxins", and QS-controlled rhamnolipids, swarming and siderophores
  "contribute to P. aeruginosa biofilm formation". de Kievit & Iglewski 2000: exotoxin A is las-regulated; a
  *lasI* mutant formed thin, flat biofilms. Wikipedia: QS controls virulence and biofilm formation; Britannica's
  summary: *P. aeruginosa* "uses quorum sensing to regulate disease mechanisms". "Holds back" means "holds back
  most of these weapons" — don't say it is harmless while few.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC101676/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing

- **"Virulence factors: the tools a microbe uses to cause disease."** — confirmed (definition, added with the
  script). OpenStax *Microbiology* 15.3: virulence factors "contribute to a pathogen's ability to cause disease";
  toxins and tissue-damaging enzymes (exoenzymes) are its standard examples.
  Sources: https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/15-3-virulence-factors-of-bacterial-and-viral-pathogens

- **Joke: "Scientists think waiting may keep it from being noticed too early — attack with a few cells, and the
  immune system wins. Patience, it turns out, is a virulence factor."** — soften (keep the joke, hedge the
  reason). The "avoid alerting the host" idea is a hypothesis: de Kievit & Iglewski write "It has been theorized"
  that toxic, immune-activating factors are made "only after a sufficient number of bacteria have been amassed",
  and if made prematurely "the host may recognize the invading bacteria sooner". Rutherford & Bassler do not state
  it. The *fact* under the joke — that the QS switch matters for disease — is solid (QS mutants are attenuated in
  animal models, per de Kievit & Iglewski). The "scientists think" must come before the punchline.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC101676/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/

- **"*Vibrio cholerae*, the comma, does the opposite. When there are few of them, they stick to the gut wall and
  make their toxin. When the crowd gets big, the same switch turns the toxin and the sticking off — and scientists
  think that helps them let go and leave."** — confirmed, with a hedge on "leave". Hammer & Bassler 2003: unlike
  many pathogens, *V. cholerae* represses virulence and biofilm at high cell density. Zhu et al. 2002 (PNAS): QS
  (HapR) "appears to repress" ToxR-regulated virulence genes (cholera toxin, TCP); they *propose* that repressing
  the TCP pilus and making HA/protease "might promote detachment". Rutherford & Bassler: at high density,
  repression of biofilms and virulence "facilitates dispersal". Walton et al. 2023: RpoS is needed to "detach from
  mucus" at high density. Detachment is a model, not directly shown in the host — keep "scientists think".
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC122484/, https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/quorum-sensing-controls-biofilm-formation-in-vibrio-cholerae/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543102/, https://frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1178538/full

- **Joke: "And its way out of the body is the disease itself: the toxin it made earlier causes the diarrhoea that
  carries it out."** — confirmed. Nelson et al. 2009: cholera toxin "is the key factor responsible for the profuse
  secretory diarrhoea"; rice-water stool carries 10^10–10^12 vibrios per litre. Walton 2023: "Bacteria are
  disseminated from the intestine with the diarrhea". CDC: people catch it from food or water containing "traces
  of poop from someone with cholera". Wikipedia (*V. cholerae*): spread from "the feces of an infected person".
  Keep "the toxin it made earlier" — the toxin is made at low density, so the exit is the result of the earlier
  stage, not of the high-density switch. About the microbe only; no patients on the board.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3842031/, https://frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1178538/full, https://www.cdc.gov/cholera/about/index.html, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrio_cholerae

- **"Same switch, opposite decision."** — confirmed (Hammer & Bassler: *V. cholerae* represses at high density
  "unlike many pathogens"; *P. aeruginosa* activates).

## F. Jamming, and where the picture breaks (step 6)

- **"Some bacteria make enzymes that cut the AHL signal apart. One, called AiiA, was found in a *Bacillus*:
  put its gene into a plant-rotting bacterium, and the rot is much weaker."** — confirmed. Dong et al. 2000
  (PNAS): *Bacillus* sp. 240B1 inactivates AHLs; AiiA expressed in *Erwinia carotovora* "attenuates pathogenicity
  on potato, eggplant, Chinese cabbage, carrot, celery, cauliflower, and tobacco". It is a lactonase (opens the
  lactone ring; Dong et al. 2001 *Nature*). Wikipedia: "enzymes called lactonases that can target and inactivate
  AHLs". Call it **quorum quenching**.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC16273/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_sensing

- **"A red seaweed from Australian waters, *Delisea pulchra*, makes molecules shaped like the signal, which jam
  the receiver — and its surface stays remarkably clean of bacteria."** — confirmed. Manefield et al. 2000:
  *D. pulchra*'s halogenated furanone "inhibited the luminescence phenotype without affecting the growth" and is
  thought to displace AHL from LuxR (a 2002 study found it speeds LuxR breakdown instead — so "jam the receiver"
  is safe, "takes its place" is not). de Nys et al. 2006: *D. pulchra* is a model for "natural antifoulants";
  furanones "inhibit bacterial colonisation and biofilm development". Wikipedia (*Delisea pulchra*): furanones are
  "structurally similar" to AHLs, "disabling bacterial communication", and the seaweed "can prevent biofilm
  accumulation on its surface"; found in southern Australia, New Zealand and further south. Note: jamming is not
  killing — it blocks behaviour, not growth.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC101458/, https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/3627/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delisea_pulchra

- **Joke "the seaweed was doing it for millions of years before us"** — drop the "millions of years". I found no
  source dating the trait. Safe replacement: **"The seaweed got there first. Chemists have since made more than two
  hundred copies of its molecules."** (de Nys et al. 2006: a library of over 200 furanone analogues.)
  Sources: https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/3627/

- **"Drugs that jam the signal instead of killing the bacteria are being studied. A few have been tried in small
  trials, but none is a standard treatment yet."** — confirmed as hedged. A phase 2 trial of azithromycin "as a
  quorum-sensing inhibitor" to prevent *P. aeruginosa* ventilator pneumonia was terminated early for financial
  reasons (92 patients; NCT00610623). A 2026 review: "no Agr-targeted antivirulence inhibitors have advanced to
  clinical application". A 2024 review: in-vitro success has not translated into clinical use; multi-phase trials
  still needed. Wikipedia names no QS inhibitor in clinical use. **Don't say "no human trials" (there was one) and
  don't say "approved".**
  Sources: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00610623, https://www.dovepress.com/research-progress-on-antivirulence-agents-targeting-the-accessory-gene-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-IDR, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1366760/full

- **"Where the 'counting' picture breaks: bacteria don't count heads. They sense how concentrated the signal is.
  A few cells in a tight space can reach the threshold, and a big crowd in open, flowing water may never reach
  it. Some scientists think the signal may tell a cell as much about its surroundings as about its
  neighbours."** — confirmed as hedged. Redfield 2002 (*Trends Microbiol.* 10:365) proposed "diffusion sensing":
  cells may use autoinducers to judge whether secreted molecules would be lost to diffusion and mixing. West et
  al. 2012 ("Quorum sensing and the confusion about diffusion"): the two ideas are "not diametrically opposed" and
  data support both social interactions and a role for diffusion. I could not open Redfield's full text (the PDF
  would not extract) — cite the idea, not quotations.
  Sources: https://zoology.ubc.ca/~redfield/PDF/RedfieldQuorum.pdf, https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/quorum-sensing-and-the-confusion-about-diffusion/

- **"In one experiment, scientists sealed single *Staphylococcus aureus* cells inside tiny droplets — and a lone
  cell switched on its quorum-sensing genes all by itself."** — confirmed. Carnes et al., *Nature Chemical
  Biology* 6:41–45 (online Nov 2009, issue Jan 2010): "we show quorum sensing and genetic re-programming to occur
  in a single isolated organism", using *agr* reporters, in droplets about 20 µm across; they compare the
  confinement to a cell trapped in an endosome/phagosome; QS cells survived better than *agr* mutants. Organism is
  *S. aureus* (as the plan says). Year: "2010" (journal issue) is standard, but "published at the end of 2009"
  also appears — avoid saying a year on air. Some droplets held 2–8 cells; the single-cell result is the claim.
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4201857/

## Dark-humour lines

- **Step 1: "The bacteria pay their rent in light. Every dawn, the squid evicts most of them anyway."** —
  confirmed. Light for counter-illumination; the squid supplies nutrients and shelter (Wikipedia *A. fischeri*);
  dawn venting of ~90–95% (above). The "rent" metaphor maps correctly (light ↔ room and board).
- **Step 5: "Patience, it turns out, is a virulence factor."** — keep, only after a hedged "scientists think
  waiting may keep it from being noticed too early" (de Kievit & Iglewski: "It has been theorized").
- **Step 5: "Its way out of the body is the disease itself."** — confirmed (toxin → diarrhoea → vibrios carried
  out; Nelson 2009, Walton 2023). Strongest of the dark lines; said calmly, about the microbe.
- **Step 6: seaweed** — "millions of years" dropped; "The seaweed got there first" + "200 copies" is safe but is
  more a wry aside than a joke. Recommend the script uses step 1, step 5 (cholera) and possibly step 5 (patience).

## Watch-outs

1. **Name: *Aliivibrio fischeri*, "once called *Vibrio fischeri*".** Wikipedia titles it *Aliivibrio fischeri*;
   most squid papers still say *Vibrio*. Say both once, then "the squid's bacteria".
2. **Venting share: never a single number.** Wikipedia's squid article says "up to 95%", its *A. fischeri*
   article "about 90%". Say "most of them" / "around nine in ten or more"; board "~90–95%".
3. **Not "every cell switches on together".** Single cells vary; say the population/crowd switches on.
4. **"*Vibrio* uses AHLs" is wrong for *V. cholerae*** (CAI-1, AI-2). Board: "squid bacteria, *Pseudomonas*: AHL".
5. **AHL diffusion is not universal:** the squid bacteria's 3-oxo-C6-HSL diffuses; *Pseudomonas*'s 3-oxo-C12-HSL
   is pumped out. Draw the dot crossing the membrane only in the squid-bacterium cell (region C).
6. **Peptide signals are mostly caught at the surface** (two-component receptors); a few streptococcal peptides
   are imported. Say "mostly".
7. **AI-2: "made by many species" is fact; "shared word between species" is a debated hypothesis.** Never
   "universal language".
8. **"Patience" joke needs the hedge first** — the immune-evasion reason is a theory.
9. **Cholera detachment is a model ("scientists think")**; and the toxin that causes the diarrhoea is made at the
   *low*-density stage — say "the toxin it made earlier".

## Correction after judge pass 1 (2026-11-11)

- **WRONG, removed: "That loop is the 'auto' in autoinducer."** The section C entry above marked it confirmed, but its
  sources support only the loop, not the naming. "Autoinduction" was named by Nealson, Platt & Hastings in 1970
  because the cells condition their own medium: the inducer is self-made. *luxI*'s place in the induced operon (the
  positive loop) was worked out in the 1980s, so the name cannot come from the loop. Replacement, safe:
  **"So the more signal there is, the more signal gets made. And the cells make this inducer themselves. That's the
  'auto' in autoinducer."** Board: "loop: more signal makes more signal" + "auto = made by the cells themselves".
  Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4751818/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoinducer
- **b9 reworded:** "Their light only switched on once the cells had built up something in their own broth: a
  substance they made themselves." (the old "built it up" read as "built up the light"). In 1970 the substance was not
  yet identified, so "something … a substance they made themselves" is the strict wording (O'Toole 2016: the cells
  "conditioned" the medium).
- **Source on the naming itself (judge pass 2):** Hastings & Greenberg 1999, *J. Bacteriol.* (PMC93702): the
  phenomenon "was attributed to a substance produced by the bacteria themselves, which was therefore dubbed the
  autoinducer". This is the citation for "auto = made by the cells themselves".
